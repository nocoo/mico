This is the postmortem for the payment API outage on Tuesday. The incident lasted fifty two minutes, from 14:07 to 14:59 UTC. During that window, about sixty percent of card payments failed, and some customers saw a generic error page. No money was lost, but we violated our error budget for the quarter and we need to understand both the technical cause and the process gaps. This meeting is blameless. We are here to find system problems, not to assign personal fault.

Let's build the timeline. At 13:55, we deployed release 4.18.2, which included a small change to the connection pool settings. At 14:03, the first alert fired for elevated latency on the payment service. At 14:07, error rate crossed five percent. The on call engineer acknowledged at 14:09 and began checking dashboards. At 14:14, the team saw database connection timeouts. At 14:21, they rolled back the release, but the error rate did not recover. At 14:32, they discovered that the DNS resolver was returning a stale address for one of the primary database replicas. At 14:41, they failed over to the secondary replica and traffic recovered. At 14:59, error rate returned to baseline and the incident was closed.

The direct cause was a combination of two changes. The release reduced the connection pool timeout from five seconds to two seconds, which made the service less tolerant of slow DNS. Separately, a network change on Monday had reduced the DNS cache TTL, so the resolver was more likely to return stale records during a failover. The stale record pointed to a replica that had been taken out of service. The service kept retrying the stale address, and the shortened timeout turned a slowdown into a hard failure.

Why did the rollback not fix it? Because the DNS record was already cached, and the rollback did not flush the resolver cache. The team assumed the release was the only change. The runbook said to roll back first, then check dependencies, but it did not say to verify DNS after a rollback. That is a process gap. The on call had the authority to fail over, but they waited for the database team because the runbook described failover as a last resort. That added about nine minutes.

Detection was also slower than it should have been. The latency alert fired at 14:03, but it was a warning, not a page. The error rate alert paged at 14:07. We had no synthetic check for card payments from an external region. The dashboard showed service level error rate, but not the percentage of payment attempts that never reached the service. If we had a synthetic payment probe, we might have caught the failure at 14:05.

Customer impact. About eighteen thousand payment attempts failed. Most were retried successfully within ten minutes. Four hundred customers saw a duplicate authorization hold, which we reversed automatically by 16:30. Support received two hundred and ten tickets. The status page was updated at 14:26, nineteen minutes after the incident started. That is too slow. We need a template and a designated communications owner.

Now the contributing factors. First, the config change was not covered by a canary in the payment path. Second, the DNS dependency was not in the service map. Third, the runbook did not include cache invalidation or DNS verification. Fourth, the failover decision required a second team, which added latency. Fifth, the status page update was manual and owned by whoever was on call. Sixth, the alert thresholds were tuned for a single region, not for the payment path as a whole.

The action items need owners and dates. First, add a synthetic payment probe that runs from two regions every minute. Owner is the reliability team, due in two weeks. Second, add DNS health to the payment service dashboard and include resolver cache checks in the runbook. Owner is the platform team, due in one week. Third, change the connection pool timeout back to five seconds and add a canary that runs for thirty minutes before full rollout. Owner is the payments team, due in three days. Fourth, give the on call engineer explicit authority to fail over the database without waiting for the database team, and update the escalation policy. Owner is the engineering manager, due in one week. Fifth, add a status page template and a communications rotation. Owner is support, due in two weeks. Sixth, add a post deploy DNS verification step to the release checklist. Owner is release engineering, due in two weeks.

We also need a longer term item. The architecture has a single resolver path for all database replicas. We should add a resolver sidecar or use a service discovery mechanism that does not depend on DNS TTL. That is a larger project and needs an RFC. Owner is the platform team, due in six weeks for a design, not implementation.

What did we do well? The team communicated in the incident channel, the rollback was executed cleanly, and the database failover worked once it was attempted. The support team reversed duplicate holds quickly. We should keep the incident channel structure and the automatic hold reversal.

What will we change about the process? We will run a five minute pre deploy check for DNS and database health. We will require a canary for any change to timeouts, connection pools, or retries. We will add a single incident commander role for severity one incidents, even if the on call is also debugging. We will set a fifteen minute target for the first status page update. We will review the action items in the weekly reliability meeting until they are closed.

Let's confirm the severity. This was a severity one because it affected payments and lasted more than thirty minutes. The error budget for the quarter is now exhausted, so any new feature that touches the payment path needs a reliability review before release. That is not a punishment; it is a consequence of the budget policy we agreed on.

Finally, the follow up review is in two weeks. We will check the synthetic probe, the runbook update, the canary, and the status page template. The platform RFC is due in six weeks. If any owner cannot meet the date, they should tell me before Friday, not after the review. Thank you. This meeting is over.
