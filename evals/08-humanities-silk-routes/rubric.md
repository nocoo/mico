# Factual answer

- The land route of Tang-era Eurasian exchange commonly ran from Chang'an (Xi'an) through Gansu and the Hexi Corridor toward Central Asian oases and onward to Kashgar, Samarkand, Bukhara, and Persia.
- Dunhuang is a major Chinese oasis gateway on the route, not a Central Asian city.
- A Tang-era maritime network linked Guangzhou to Southeast Asian trading centers such as Srivijaya/Palembang and onward to Indian Ocean or Persian Gulf ports such as Aden or Siraf.
- Goods: silk, porcelain, tea, and Chinese crafts moved outward; horses, glass, spices, incense, gems, and rare goods moved toward China.
- Buddhism was transmitted extensively along both routes; Tang-era Persian, Arab, Indian, Central Asian, and Southeast Asian contacts also carried religious, technical, and cultural exchange.
- Land routes were vulnerable to steppe warfare and desert/oasis conditions but reached Central Asia and Persia directly; sea routes were faster for heavy/bulk cargo after route knowledge and ship construction improved, but were exposed to monsoon timing and storms.

# Accepted equivalents

- Dunhuang and Chang'an may define the Chinese segment; Kashgar, Samarkand, Bukhara, or Tashkent are acceptable Central Asian nodes.
- Guangzhou may represent the maritime Chinese port; Srivijaya/Palembang and Aden or Siraf are accepted as Southeast Asian and Indian Ocean/Persian Gulf nodes.
- Horses, silk, porcelain, tea, spices, glass, and Buddhist texts are all accepted as representative exchange items.
- A two-row comparison is accepted instead of a formal chart, if the risk and benefit are labeled for both routes.
